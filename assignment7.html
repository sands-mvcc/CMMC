Introduction — Access Control and CMMC

Overview

Why Access Control Is a Critical CMMC Requirement

Access control is the first and most foundational domain in NIST SP 800-171 and CMMC Level 2. It establishes who can access systems, data, and resources — and under what conditions. An improperly configured access environment is the single most common source of audit findings in DoD contractor assessments. The Access Control Matrix (ACM) is the primary artifact that maps personnel to systems and permissions, making it the backbone of any defensible CMMC compliance posture.

Why C3PAO Assessors Review Access Control Matrices

During a CMMC Level 2 assessment, a C3PAO assessor will request the organization's ACM as direct evidence of control implementation. Assessors look for documented proof that access has been assigned deliberately, reviewed periodically, and aligned with job responsibilities. An ACM that has never been reviewed, contains stale accounts, or grants excessive privileges is a clear indicator of organizational risk and will generate scored findings.

How ACMs Support Least Privilege

The principle of least privilege requires that every user, process, and system account operate with only the minimum permissions necessary to perform their assigned function. An ACM forces this question to be answered explicitly — for every person and every system — rather than allowing access to accumulate organically over time. NIST practice 3.1.1 requires limiting access to authorized users, and 3.1.5 requires employing the principle of least privilege.

How Improper Access Creates Risk to CUI

Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI) must be accessible only to personnel with a documented business need. Excessive access creates attack surface: an insider threat, compromised credential, or accidental disclosure can expose CUI to unauthorized parties. DFARS 252.204-7012 holds contractors legally responsible for protecting CUI, and a single unauthorized disclosure can result in contract termination, debarment, and criminal liability.

Why Organizations Must Regularly Review Access Permissions

Personnel roles change. Employees leave. New systems are deployed. Without a scheduled access review cycle, stale permissions accumulate silently. NIST practice 3.1.3 requires controlling the flow of CUI, and 3.1.6 requires using non-privileged accounts for non-privileged activities. Periodic access reviews — at minimum annually, and after any role change or termination — are expected by CMMC assessors as evidence of active access management.

Scenario: Federal Food Services Inc. (FFSI) manufactures MREs, emergency food kits, and military ration products for the U.S. Army. FFSI handles both Federal Contract Information (FCI) and Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI) across cloud and on-premises systems. FFSI leadership has requested a compliance team review and update the organization's Access Control Matrix prior to their upcoming CMMC Level 2 assessment. You are a member of that compliance team.

Relevant NIST SP 800-171 Access Control Practices

  • 3.1.1 Limit system access to authorized users and processes
  • 3.1.2 Limit access to types of transactions and functions authorized users are permitted to execute
  • 3.1.3 Control the flow of CUI in accordance with approved authorizations
  • 3.1.4 Separate duties of individuals to reduce risk of malevolent activity
  • 3.1.5 Employ the principle of least privilege
  • 3.1.6 Use non-privileged accounts for non-privileged activities
  • 3.1.7 Prevent non-privileged users from executing privileged functions
  • 3.1.9 Provide privacy and security notices on system logon

Key Terms

Reference Glossary
Access Control Matrix (ACM)
A structured table that maps users or roles to systems, applications, and data assets, specifying the type of access permitted (read, modify, approve, admin, or none) for each combination.
Least Privilege
The security principle requiring that users, processes, and accounts operate with only the minimum permissions necessary to perform their assigned job functions — no more.
Need-to-Know
A determination that a specific individual requires access to specific information in order to perform official duties. Access to CUI requires both authorization and need-to-know.
Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)
An access control model in which permissions are assigned to roles rather than individuals. Users receive access by being assigned to an appropriate role that matches their job function.
Privileged Account
An account with elevated permissions beyond those of a standard user — including admin rights, root access, or the ability to configure systems, manage accounts, or access all data.
Standard User
An account with typical read or modify access required for day-to-day job functions, without administrative or system-level privileges.
CUI (Controlled Unclassified Information)
Information the government creates or possesses that requires safeguarding per law, regulation, or policy. DoD contractors handling CUI must comply with NIST SP 800-171 and CMMC requirements.
FCI (Federal Contract Information)
Information provided by or generated for the government under a contract to develop or deliver a product or service. FCI requires basic safeguarding per FAR 52.204-21.
Access Authorization
The formal approval granted to a user to access a system or data type, based on a documented business justification and management approval. Authorizations must be reviewed periodically.
Separation of Duties (SoD)
A control principle requiring that no single individual control all phases of a critical process. SoD prevents fraud, error, and abuse by distributing responsibilities across multiple individuals.
User Role
A defined set of permissions and responsibilities associated with a job function. In RBAC environments, access is granted by assigning a user to one or more roles rather than by individual permission grants.
Administrative Access
Elevated system access that allows a user to configure settings, manage user accounts, install software, modify security controls, or access all system data regardless of classification.
Shared Account
An account used by more than one individual, which prevents individual accountability and makes audit trail attribution impossible. Shared accounts are a CMMC assessment finding.
Multifactor Authentication (MFA)
An authentication method requiring two or more verification factors — something you know, something you have, and/or something you are. Required for privileged accounts and CUI system access under NIST 800-171 practice 3.5.3.

Excellent · 90–100

Proficient · 75–89

Completes the ACM with minor errors or omissions. Identifies most least-privilege and SoD issues. Corrective actions are reasonable but may lack specific NIST references or named owners. Executive summary addresses key findings.

Developing · 60–74
