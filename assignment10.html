Introduction — Incident Response and CMMC

Overview
⚡ FFSI Incident Scenario — Credential Compromise via Phishing

It is Tuesday, October 14, 2025 at 9:47 AM. Marcus Webb, FFSI's Contracts Manager, receives an email that appears to originate from a DoD contracting officer at the Defense Logistics Agency. The email subject reads: "ACTION REQUIRED: FY2026 Contract Modification — FFSI-MRE-2026-0047."

Webb clicks the link in the email, which redirects him to a convincing but fraudulent DoD login portal. He enters his FFSI Microsoft 365 credentials. Within four minutes, the attacker uses those credentials to authenticate to the FFSI Contract Management System — a system that contains Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI) including contract specifications, delivery schedules, production quantities, and Army end-use statements.

At 10:23 AM, FFSI's SIEM platform generates an alert: "Anomalous authentication — Contract Management System — user mwebb — source IP: 185.220.101.47 (Tor exit node, Netherlands)." The Security Analyst on duty escalates the alert to the IT Manager. The Help Desk receives a separate call from Webb asking why his password "stopped working."

Affected systems: M365 Contract Mgmt System Active Directory VPN  ·  Data at risk: CUI Contract Data FCI Documents

Why Incident Response Is a Critical CMMC Requirement

Cybersecurity incidents are inevitable — no organization is impervious to phishing, credential theft, ransomware, or insider threats. CMMC Level 2 mandates a documented, tested, and operational Incident Response Plan as a prerequisite for certification. Without it, an organization cannot demonstrate the ability to contain damage, protect CUI, or recover operations in a controlled manner. The IR domain maps directly to three NIST SP 800-171 practices that are among the most scrutinized during C3PAO assessments.

Why Assessors Review IRPs and Incident Records

A C3PAO assessor will request the IRP as a primary artifact and will look for evidence that the plan has been exercised within the past 12 months. Assessors interview personnel to verify they know their roles. They review historical incident logs to confirm that actual incidents were handled in accordance with the plan — and that lessons learned were incorporated into policy updates. A plan that exists on paper but has never been practiced is not evidence of control implementation.

How Incident Response Protects CUI

Every minute an attacker maintains access to systems containing CUI, the scope of potential disclosure expands. Rapid detection, immediate containment, and systematic eradication — following a pre-defined IR plan — dramatically limits CUI exposure. NIST practice 3.6.1 requires establishing an operational incident-handling capability. Practice 3.6.2 requires tracking, documenting, and reporting incidents. Practice 3.6.3 requires testing the plan.

Why Documentation Is Essential

Incident documentation serves three critical purposes: it creates the audit trail a C3PAO will review; it satisfies DFARS 252.204-7012's requirement to report cyber incidents to DoD within 72 hours; and it provides the data needed for root cause analysis and corrective action planning. Undocumented incidents — even if handled effectively — will generate findings during an assessment because there is no evidence the response occurred.

Relevant NIST SP 800-171 IR Practices

  • 3.6.1 Establish an operational incident-handling capability including preparation, detection, analysis, containment, recovery, and user activities
  • 3.6.2 Track, document, and report incidents to designated officials and/or authorities both internal and external to the organization
  • 3.6.3 Test the organizational incident response capability
DFARS Requirement: Under DFARS 252.204-7012, defense contractors must report cyber incidents affecting CUI to the DoD Cyber Crime Center (DC3) within 72 hours of discovery. Failure to report is a contract compliance violation independent of the technical incident itself.

Key Terms

Reference Glossary
Incident Response Plan (IRP)
A documented set of procedures defining how an organization detects, analyzes, contains, eradicates, recovers from, and reports cybersecurity incidents. Required by NIST 800-171 practice 3.6.1 and evaluated by C3PAO assessors as primary IR domain evidence.
Cybersecurity Incident
An event that actually or potentially compromises the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of information or an information system — or that constitutes a violation or imminent threat of violation of security policies.
Security Event
Any observable occurrence in a system or network that may have security implications but has not been confirmed as a breach or policy violation. All incidents begin as events, but not all events escalate to incidents.
Containment
Actions taken to limit the spread or impact of an active incident — including isolating affected systems, revoking compromised credentials, blocking malicious IPs, or disabling affected accounts — while preserving evidence for analysis.
Eradication
The process of removing the root cause of an incident from all affected systems — including malware removal, patching exploited vulnerabilities, closing unauthorized access paths, and verifying no persistence mechanisms remain.
Recovery
The process of restoring affected systems and operations to normal, verified-clean status after eradication — including restoring from backups, re-enabling services, resetting credentials, and confirming normal operations.
Root Cause Analysis
A structured investigation to identify the fundamental reason an incident occurred — not just the immediate trigger, but the underlying technical or procedural vulnerabilities that enabled it — enabling effective corrective action.
CUI (Controlled Unclassified Information)
Government-created or -possessed information requiring safeguarding per law, regulation, or policy. DoD contractors handling CUI must comply with NIST SP 800-171 and CMMC requirements. Any incident touching CUI triggers mandatory DoD reporting.
FCI (Federal Contract Information)
Information provided by or generated for the government under a contract. While less sensitive than CUI, FCI exposure in a breach context still requires documentation and may require disclosure depending on contract terms.
Incident Handler
A designated individual with assigned responsibilities in the incident response process — such as the Incident Coordinator, Technical Lead, or Evidence Custodian. CMMC requires that IR roles be defined in advance and trained.
Evidence Preservation
The process of collecting, securing, and documenting forensic artifacts — logs, memory captures, disk images, and network captures — in a manner that maintains their integrity and admissibility for legal or regulatory proceedings.
Chain of Custody
A documented record tracking who collected, handled, transferred, and accessed digital evidence — ensuring its integrity and establishing that it has not been altered or tampered with since collection.
Escalation
The formal process of elevating an incident to higher organizational authority — management, legal, or external agencies — based on severity, CUI involvement, or regulatory obligations. Escalation paths must be defined in the IRP before an incident occurs.
Breach Notification
The legal or regulatory obligation to notify affected parties — including DoD via DC3 within 72 hours under DFARS 252.204-7012 — when a cyber incident affects CUI or other protected information. Failure to notify is an independent compliance violation.
Lessons Learned
A structured post-incident review that identifies what worked, what failed, and what controls should be improved or added. Lessons learned must be documented and used to update the IRP — assessors look for evidence that the plan evolves based on actual incidents.

Performance Level Descriptions

Excellent · 90–100

Accurately classifies the incident and severity with scenario-specific evidence. Completes all IRP fields with specific, actionable entries. Assigns all 6 IRT roles with justified selections. Applies all 6 lifecycle phases with FFSI-specific actions. Produces a rigorous root cause analysis with prioritized, NIST-cited corrective actions.

Proficient · 75–89

Correctly classifies the incident with adequate justification. Completes most IRP fields with only minor omissions. Assigns IRT roles with reasonable rationale. Addresses all 6 lifecycle phases. Root cause identified; corrective actions are reasonable but may lack NIST citations or specific ownership.

Developing · 60–74

Incident classified but severity misjudged or justification is thin. IRP fields partially completed; some required fields blank. IRT assignments made but some roles missing or poorly justified. Lifecycle phases present but actions are generic. Root cause vague; corrective actions lack priority or ownership.
