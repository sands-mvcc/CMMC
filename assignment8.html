Introduction — Audit Logging & Monitoring Under CMMC

Overview

Why Audit Logs Are Critical During a CMMC Assessment

Audit logs are the evidentiary backbone of a CMMC Level 2 assessment. A C3PAO assessor cannot verify that security controls are operating as described in the System Security Plan without examining logs that demonstrate those controls are active, alerting, and being reviewed. Every claim in the SSP — from "we monitor privileged account activity" to "we detect unauthorized access attempts" — must be substantiated with log data. Organizations that lack logging, retain logs insufficiently, or fail to review them actively will generate findings in multiple CMMC domains simultaneously.

Why Organizations Must Actively Review Logs

Generating logs is necessary but insufficient. NIST practice 3.3.1 requires creating and retaining audit logs to enable the monitoring, analysis, investigation, and reporting of unlawful or unauthorized system activity. Practice 3.3.2 requires that designated personnel review and update logged events. A SIEM that generates alerts nobody reads is not evidence of monitoring — it is evidence of a monitoring failure. Assessors will ask who reviews logs, how often, and what they do with findings.

Why Assessors Request Audit Evidence

During assessment, C3PAO assessors use audit records to validate three things: (1) that the described controls are technically active, (2) that the organization detects anomalies when they occur, and (3) that detected anomalies are acted upon. They will request SIEM screenshots, log exports, alert-response tickets, and the Audit Log & Monitoring Report itself. Gaps in any of these create scored deficiencies — even if the underlying controls are functioning correctly.

How Monitoring Supports Incident Detection

Monitoring and incident response are interdependent. Without monitoring, incidents go undetected until damage is catastrophic. The FFSI phishing incident (IR Exercise) was detected by SIEM monitoring — without that alert at 10:23 AM, the attacker might have operated undetected for days. NIST practice 3.14.6 (SI domain) requires monitoring organizational systems to detect attacks and indicators of potential attacks. Monitoring is what converts the IRP from a theoretical document into an operational capability.

Why Log Review Activities Must Be Documented

The act of reviewing logs must itself be logged. An assessor asking "do you review your logs?" is satisfied by neither a verbal "yes" nor a single screenshot. They expect to see a recurring, documented Audit Log & Monitoring Report — a structured record showing what was reviewed, by whom, when, what was found, and what was done about it. That document is what you are completing in this exercise.

Scenario: FFSI's Compliance Manager has tasked the Security Analyst with completing the monthly Audit Log & Monitoring Report covering the period of October 1–31, 2025. The SIEM has flagged 10 events for analyst review this month. Your job is to triage these events, classify their severity, determine whether escalation is required, and complete the ALMR.

Relevant NIST SP 800-171 Practices

  • 3.3.1 Create and retain system audit logs to enable monitoring, analysis, investigation, and reporting of unlawful or unauthorized activity
  • 3.3.2 Ensure the actions of individual system users can be uniquely traced to those users so they can be held accountable
  • 3.3.5 Correlate audit record review, analysis, and reporting processes for investigation and response to indications of unlawful, unauthorized, suspicious, or unusual activity
  • 3.3.6 Provide audit record reduction and report generation to support on-demand analysis and reporting
  • 3.14.6 Monitor organizational systems, including inbound and outbound communications traffic, to detect attacks and indicators of potential attacks
  • 3.14.7 Identify unauthorized use of organizational systems

Key Terms

Reference Glossary
Audit Log
A chronological, tamper-evident record of system events — including user logins, access attempts, configuration changes, file operations, and privileged actions — generated automatically by systems and applications. Required by NIST 800-171 practice 3.3.1.
Security Event
Any observable occurrence in a network or system that may have security implications — ranging from routine authentication attempts to suspicious access patterns. Events are analyzed to determine whether they represent incidents.
Security Incident
A confirmed event that violates or imminently threatens to violate security policies, acceptable use policies, or standard security practices — requiring an active response per the Incident Response Plan.
SIEM
Security Information and Event Management — a platform that aggregates log data from multiple sources, correlates events across systems, generates alerts based on defined rules, and provides centralized audit record storage and search capability.
Log Correlation
The process of combining and analyzing log records from multiple systems or time periods to detect patterns that no single source could reveal — such as a failed login followed by a successful login from a different IP to a CUI system.
Event Monitoring
The continuous or periodic review of log data and security alerts to identify anomalies, policy violations, and indicators of attack. Monitoring must be active and documented — passive log storage without review does not satisfy CMMC requirements.
Alert
An automated notification generated by a SIEM, EDR, or monitoring tool when observed activity matches a defined suspicious or malicious pattern. Alerts require analyst review and documented disposition — unreviewed alerts are a CMMC finding.
Audit Review
The structured, recurring examination of audit logs and SIEM alerts by designated personnel to identify security anomalies and verify control effectiveness. The Audit Log & Monitoring Report documents this review activity.
Audit Trail
A complete, unbroken sequence of log records that establishes accountability for a sequence of actions — enabling forensic reconstruction of what happened, when, by whom, and on which system. Required to support incident investigation under 3.3.5.
Privileged Account
An account with elevated system rights — administrative, root, or service accounts — whose activities present heightened risk. Privileged account actions must be logged, individually attributed, and reviewed more frequently than standard user activity.
Failed Login Attempt
An authentication failure recorded in system logs. Isolated failures are normal; patterns of repeated failures, failures followed by success, or failures against multiple accounts in rapid sequence are indicators of brute-force or credential-stuffing attacks.
Security Monitoring
The organizational capability to continuously observe system activity, network traffic, and user behavior for anomalies. Monitoring encompasses tooling (SIEM, EDR, firewall logs), people (analysts), and process (alert triage, escalation, documentation).
Retention Period
The defined minimum duration for which audit logs must be preserved. NIST 800-171 requires sufficient retention to support incident investigation; DFARS guidance suggests a minimum of 90 days online retention plus 3 years archival for CUI system logs.
Log Integrity
The assurance that audit logs have not been altered, deleted, or tampered with since creation. Log integrity is protected through write-once storage, cryptographic hashing, and separation of duties between system administrators and log custodians.
CUI
Controlled Unclassified Information — government-created or -possessed information requiring protection per NIST SP 800-171. Any security event affecting systems containing CUI receives elevated priority and may trigger DFARS reporting obligations.
