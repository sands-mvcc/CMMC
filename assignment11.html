Introduction — Vulnerability Management & CMMC Overview
Why vulnerability scanning is critical to CMMC compliance and CUI protection
▼

Why Vulnerability Management Is Critical to CMMC Compliance

Unpatched vulnerabilities are the most common initial access vector in defense contractor breaches. NIST SP 800-171 practices 3.14.1 and 3.14.2 require organizations to identify, report, and correct information system flaws — and to provide protection from malicious code. CMMC Level 2 assessors treat an absent or irregular vulnerability scanning program as a scored deficiency, even if the underlying systems are otherwise well-configured. Scanning must be performed, results must be documented, and remediation must be tracked.

Why Assessors Review Vulnerability Reports

A C3PAO assessor will request the Vulnerability Scanning Report as primary evidence for the SI domain. They will examine scan frequency, scope (are all CUI systems included?), severity findings, remediation timelines, and whether the organization can demonstrate that findings were actually addressed. The VSR is also cross-referenced against the Risk Register, POA&M, and Configuration Management Plan. A scan report that shows critical vulnerabilities with no corresponding remediation entries is a major finding.

How Vulnerabilities Increase Risk to CUI

Every unpatched vulnerability on a CUI system is a potential exfiltration pathway. A critical remote code execution vulnerability on the Contract Management System, for example, could give an attacker full access to Army contract data, production specifications, and delivery schedules. DFARS 252.204-7012 holds FFSI legally responsible for protecting this data — a breach resulting from a known, unpatched vulnerability is not only a security failure but a contractual and regulatory violation.

Why Remediation Must Be Documented

Remediating a vulnerability without documenting the action leaves no audit trail. Assessors cannot verify a patch was applied if there is no dated maintenance record, change request, or configuration update. NIST practice 3.4.3 requires tracking and logging system changes — applying a patch is a configuration change that requires documentation. Vulnerabilities that cannot be immediately remediated must be entered into the POA&M with a risk acceptance decision and compensating controls identified.

Learning Objectives 6 Objectives
What you will be able to do upon completing this exercise
▼
1
Explain how NIST SP 800-171 SI domain requirements (3.14.1–3.14.7) define the vulnerability management obligations of a CMMC Level 2 defense contractor and what evidence C3PAO assessors examine.
2
Interpret 10 realistic vulnerability scan findings — analyzing CVE identifiers, CVSS scores, exploit availability, and CUI system scope — to determine the business and security risk of each finding.
3
Assess asset criticality across 10 FFSI systems, determining which assets warrant the highest remediation priority based on CUI exposure, operational impact, and attack surface.
4
Complete a formal Vulnerability Scanning Report with all required fields, including risk rating, remediation action, target date, and assigned owner for each finding.
5
Develop a three-tier remediation plan (0–30, 30–60, 60–90 days) that sequences vulnerability remediation by risk priority, operational constraints, and CUI impact.
6
Prepare an executive vulnerability summary suitable for FFSI leadership briefing — addressing overall posture, CUI risk, assessment readiness, and priority actions before the CMMC assessment.
